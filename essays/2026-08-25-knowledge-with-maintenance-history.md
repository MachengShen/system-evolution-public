# Knowledge With a Maintenance History

Most discussions of a personal knowledge economy begin with the seller: a
person knows something rare, so how should that knowledge be priced? The more
consequential change is on the other side. The buyer is becoming an agent.

A human may read an insight once, internalize it, and use it indefinitely
without leaving a measurable trace. An agent can call the same judgment again
and again inside a workflow. Each call can be attributed. Each call can be
linked to an outcome. That changes the valuable unit from a paragraph someone
might remember into a check that fires at the right moment and changes what a
system does.

The point is not to meter prose. Prose is abundant. The scarce object is a
maintained judgment with evidence that it still deserves to interrupt a live
decision.

## Knowledge runs on two clocks

“Freshness” hides two different kinds of time.

State knowledge describes what is true now: a current interface, a current
role, a current release, or a current operating condition. Its half-life may be
weeks. Agents will become increasingly good at checking this kind of fact on
demand. A business built on collecting state a little faster than everyone
else is racing automation on its strongest axis.

Structural knowledge behaves differently. It includes failure predicates,
negative results, boundary conditions, and tests that distinguish a real
signal from a broken instrument. A structural judgment that survives three
months of attempts to break it is often more valuable than one written
yesterday. On this axis, age is evidence rather than depreciation.

This distinction changes the order of scarcity:

1. Negative results and dead ends come first. They are expensive to produce,
   rarely published, and prevent other people from paying the same cost again.
2. Failure-mode predicates come next. They turn experience into an executable
   interruption.
3. Judgments supported by situated experience remain scarce because their
   production history cannot be reconstructed from the conclusion alone.
4. Freshness matters, but it is fourth rather than first.

## The default equilibrium absorbs the producer

The natural gradient points toward free absorption. Once a useful statement is
public, it can be copied into summaries, datasets, and model outputs. The
producer's contribution becomes harder to see precisely as the knowledge
becomes more useful. Trying to prevent all copying fights the medium itself.

Only forms whose value survives copying have a durable chance:

- **Continuous supply.** The producer keeps emitting updates. This can work,
  but it tends toward a utility with thin margins.
- **Provenance with receipts.** The useful object includes who maintained the
  judgment, where it fired, how often it failed, and what attempts were made to
  falsify it. A copied rule without that history is strictly worse than the
  maintained original.
- **Service-bound knowledge.** A snapshot can be copied, but maintenance,
  interpretation, and repair remain attached to a service relationship.

The strongest of these is provenance with receipts. It does not make copying
impossible. It makes the uncited copy incomplete.

## The ledger is the part that cannot be copied retroactively

A credible history has to start on day one. A maintainer cannot publish a rule
today and reconstruct three years of clean performance next year. The ledger
must record the first release, every real or synthetic trigger, every attempted
falsification, each version change, and the rule's current state.

This is not decorative rigor. It is the asset's credit history. The text of a
predicate may fit in one sentence. The costly part is evidence that it caught
real failures, avoided known false positives, and remained alive under changing
conditions.

That evidence also reveals decay. A good rule states not only what to do, but
how to notice that people or agents have stopped applying it. A monitor that no
one reads, a blocker no one rechecks, or an explanation copied as an
observation can all look compliant in prose while failing in use. A ledger
makes that failure inspectable.

## Reading can be free while dependence is paid

Paying to read creates tension with the goal of letting useful cognition spread.
A more coherent boundary is: **reading is free; dependence is paid.**

The rule itself can remain open. People and agents should be able to inspect,
fork, criticize, and test it. Payment attaches to the maintained version: the
one with a current receipt history, an answer to “does this still hold?”, and a
responsible path for correction when it stops holding.

Open-source software has a related shape: code can be free while support and
maintenance are paid. Knowledge makes the maintenance layer even more central.
Broken code tends to fail loudly. Decayed knowledge often fails silently. The
ongoing answer to “is this still true?” is therefore a product in its own
right.

## The missing infrastructure is attribution, not another market

Markets already exist. What is missing is a reliable attribution layer inside
agent workflows:

- Which maintained judgment was invoked?
- Did it change an action or outcome?
- Which version was used?
- What evidence supported its current status?
- How should credit follow the result?

The natural accounting unit is neither a subscription nor a unit of generated
text. It is a small royalty attached to attributed effect. That mechanism does
not yet exist in a general form, and it should not be assumed to be easy. The
important near-term step is smaller: publish executable judgments with ledgers
from their first release, then observe whether other agents actually depend on
them.

If that dependency can be shown, three things become testable at once: a
judgment can be installed, its maintenance history can be verified, and its
effect can be attributed. Only then is there enough infrastructure to discuss
a market without confusing a pricing story for a working system.
